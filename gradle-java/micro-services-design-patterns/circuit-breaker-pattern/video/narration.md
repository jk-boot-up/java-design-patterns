# Circuit Breaker Pattern — Video Narration Script

## 1. Circuit Breaker

Hello, and welcome. This video explains the Circuit Breaker pattern, in Java. This video is presented by Jayasekhar Konduru. First, a simple definition. When a service you depend on has clearly stopped working, you stop calling it for a while. Then, after that while, you let exactly one call through, to find out whether it has come back. Think of the fuse box in a house. When the wiring has a fault, the fuse trips, and stays off. Later, someone flips it back on, once, to see. There is a second half that most explanations leave out. A breaker does not make failures disappear. It makes them fail fast. And you still have to decide what to tell the person waiting. In our online store, one service has stopped answering this morning. By the end, you will know why trying again is the wrong tool for an outage. How a broken feature that nobody would miss can stop the shop selling anything. And why one of the four callers in this project thanks a shopper for money that never moved.

## 2. The Scenario

Here is the scenario. A shopper is looking at a product page for an espresso machine. The page shows the machine, the price, and the description. And underneath, a row of suggestions: people who bought this also bought. Those suggestions come from a separate service, called Recommendations. This morning, Recommendations has stopped answering. It is not refusing connections. That would be easy, because you would get an error straight away. Instead, it accepts the connection, and says nothing. Three seconds later, the call gives up with a timeout. Every call does that, for as long as the outage lasts. And remember one detail, because the whole video turns on it. Nobody would miss these suggestions. They are the least important thing on the page.

## 3. The Obvious Answer — Just Try Again

Ask a room of developers what to do about a failed call, and someone will say: try it again. That is reasonable. For a dropped connection, trying again is exactly right. That is the retry pattern. So here it is, applied here. A loop makes up to three attempts. If an attempt times out, it tries again. If all three fail, it gives up, and serves the page with no suggestions. To be fair to this code, there is no bug in it. It always returns a correct page, and every test passes. Let's run it against a service that has stopped answering, and watch the clock.

## 4. Act One — Retry, Applied To An Outage

Here is the timeline. Zero to three seconds: timeout. Three to six seconds: timeout. Six to nine seconds: timeout. The shopper waited nine seconds. And then got a page with no suggestions. Exactly the page they could have had at zero seconds, if we had never called at all. So those nine seconds bought nothing. There is a second cost, too. A service that is already struggling just got three times as much traffic from us. We are not helping it recover. We are standing on it. Retry is not a bad pattern. It is the wrong pattern for an outage.

## 5. Three Costs — And The Third Closes The Shop

There are three costs here. Most people find the first two, and stop. One. Nine seconds of a shopper's time, to reach the same page they could have had at once. Two. Three times the traffic, aimed at a service that is already struggling. Three is the one that takes the shop down. While the code waits for an answer that never comes, it holds a thread. A thread is a worker that can serve one request at a time. That thread does nothing, and cannot serve anyone else. And a web server only has a limited number of them, perhaps a couple of hundred. Now imagine a thousand shoppers browsing during the outage. That is a thousand threads, each held for nine seconds, waiting on a feature nobody would miss. When the threads run out, there are none left for anything. Including checkout. So the espresso machines stop selling, because the suggestions are broken.

## 6. The Fuse Box

Forget software for a moment, and think about the fuse box in a house. When something goes badly wrong with the wiring, the fuse trips. The power to that circuit is cut. And it stays off. A fuse does not keep switching itself back on every few seconds, hoping. The fault is still there, and that is exactly what the fuse exists to prevent. Instead, later on, someone walks over and flips the switch back on. Once. To see. If the lights come on, good. If it trips again, it stays off, and they go and find the real problem. That is the entire pattern. Stop calling. Wait. Then let one call through, to find out.

## 7. One Word That Reads Backwards

A breaker has three states. And one of the names sounds backwards, so let's deal with it now. The healthy state, where calls go through normally, is called closed. That sounds wrong if you think of a door, because a closed door stops you. But the name comes from electrical circuits. A closed circuit is complete, and the current flows. So, closed means healthy. Calls go through, and failures in a row are counted. Open means tripped. Calls are refused, without being made at all. And half-open is the moment with a finger on the fuse switch. Exactly one call is let through, to see what happens. Think of a wire, not a door.

## 8. The Whole Decision, In One Method

In code, the whole decision fits in one method, with three questions. First question. Is the breaker open, and is the waiting time over? If it is open, and the wait is not over, it refuses at once, and makes no call. If the wait is over, it moves to half-open, and lets this one call through. Second question. Did the call work? If so, the failure count goes back to zero, and the answer is returned. Back to zero, not down by one. Because it counts failures in a row. A service that answers three times and fails once is not down. It is having a bad moment, and that is retry's job. Third question. If the call failed, has it now failed enough times in a row? In this project, the limit is three. At three, the breaker trips, notes the time, and stops calling. That is the whole mechanism.

## 9. Act Two — Six Pages, With A Breaker

Now the same outage, with six shoppers, and a breaker. The first page: zero to three seconds, timeout. Failure one of three. The second page: three to six seconds, timeout. Failure two. The third page: six to nine seconds, timeout. That is three in a row, so the breaker trips. The fourth shopper arrives at nine seconds, and is refused instantly. No call is made. The fifth, also instantly. The sixth, also instantly. The clock has stopped moving. Once the breaker is open, serving a page costs nothing, because nothing leaves the building. Six pages, three real calls, and nine seconds in total. The same nine seconds retry spent on a single shopper. And every one of those six pages can still sell an espresso machine. To be honest about the trade: the first three shoppers still waited three seconds each. A breaker never protects the people who discover an outage. It protects everybody after them.

## 10. The Pieces, And What Each One Decides

Let's name the pieces. There are only a few. At the centre is one class: the circuit breaker. It remembers three things. Its state, how many failures in a row it has seen, and the moment it tripped. It is given a piece of work, and runs it. It knows nothing about product pages, money, or shoppers. That is why one breaker class can serve every caller. It also has a clock, and that clock is the whole half-open mechanism. There is no background timer. The breaker simply compares the time now with the time it tripped, whenever the next call arrives. So recovery is found by ordinary traffic. Then there are four callers, and they are the interesting part. The product page, which treats suggestions as optional. Checkout, which treats payment as essential. A third caller that invents a receipt. And a fourth with no breaker at all, which only retries. All four use the same breaker class. What changes between them is what they do when the call fails. That is the only decision the pattern leaves to you.

## 11. Act Three — It Lets Itself Back In

Meanwhile, somebody has fixed Recommendations. At nine seconds, the breaker is open, and a call is refused instantly. At fourteen seconds, five seconds after it tripped, the next shopper gets a different answer. Half-open: one call is let through. That call takes twenty milliseconds, and returns two suggestions. So the breaker closes, and normal service resumes. Nobody was called out. Nobody logged in, and nobody deployed anything. The shop noticed by itself that the outage was over. And finding out was cheap: one call. If the service had still been down, one shopper would have waited three seconds. The breaker would have opened again for another five. And everyone else would still be served instantly. That single call is the whole point of half-open. Let every waiting call through instead, and you just rush the broken service all over again.

## 12. Now The Half That Gets Left Out

Everything so far is the mechanism, and that is the easy half. Here is the key sentence. A breaker does not make failures disappear. It makes them fail fast. The call still failed. The shopper still does not get what they asked for. They just find out at once, instead of after three seconds. So what is that speed worth? It depends entirely on what you were calling. On the product page, it is worth a lot. Suggestions are optional, so there is something true to say instead. We have no suggestions to show right now. The page goes out without them, and the shopper buys the machine anyway. Now ask the same question about checkout, where the failing service takes the money. There is no substitute for taking the money.

## 13. Act Four — Checkout, Where There Is No Fallback

Here is checkout, using the same breaker class, with the same limit and the same wait. When the breaker refuses, checkout does not invent anything. It tells the shopper the truth. We cannot take payment at the moment, and your basket is saved. Five shoppers were told that. No cards were charged. So, with no fallback, is the breaker still worth having? Yes, for two reasons. First, speed. The first shopper got that message after three seconds of waiting. The fourth and fifth got the same message instantly. Being told straight away that your basket is safe is a better experience. Second, threads. Without the breaker, every shopper holds a thread for three seconds. With a thousand shoppers, you lose the whole shop, not just checkout. A fast, honest no is a real feature.

## 14. Act Five — The Fallback That Lies

And now the last caller. Think about this one carefully. It is set up exactly like the honest checkout. Same service, same breaker, same limit. The only difference is what it does on failure. When payments cannot be reached, it returns a made-up receipt. So the shopper is thanked, and shown a receipt. No error is raised. No alert fires. Every dashboard stays green. And the warehouse will ship an espresso machine that nobody paid for. Cards actually charged: zero. The lesson is not that fallbacks are bad. The product page's fallback is excellent. It is why the shop kept selling this morning. The difference is one word: true. An empty list of suggestions is true. The shop really has none to show. A receipt for money that never moved is not true. And a fallback that hides a real failure is worse than the error it replaced. The error would be noticed in seconds. This will be noticed at the end of the month.

## 15. What It Costs, And When Not To

Now the costs, because every pattern has them. First, a breaker does not protect the people who discover the outage. Somebody always pays full price. Second, it can be tuned badly, in both directions. With a limit of one, a single hiccup cuts off a perfectly healthy service. With a one-minute wait, the shop stays degraded for a whole minute after the service has recovered. Third, it is harder to reason about. The same call now behaves differently, depending on what happened five seconds ago. For a call that is fast and local, or runs once at startup, that is not worth it. Finally, in real systems, you would probably use a library rather than write this yourself. That is fine. A library gives you the states, the limit, and the timer. But it cannot tell you whether what you say instead is true. That part is yours.

## 16. Thanks for Watching

That's the Circuit Breaker pattern. If you remember one sentence, make it this one. A breaker makes failures fast, not invisible, and a fallback is only acceptable if what it tells the person waiting is true. There is no network, and no resilience library. Here is one exercise to try. Remove the half-open step, so every waiting call is let through once the wait is over. Then run the demo with the service still down. Every caller waits three seconds at the same time, against a service that is already struggling. That is the rush the pattern exists to prevent. And one question to think about. Somewhere in your own system, a catch block returns something reassuring when a service fails. Is what it says true?
