Sidecar Pattern — Video Narration Script

1. Sidecar

Hello, and welcome. This video explains the Sidecar pattern, in Java. This video is presented by Jayasekhar Konduru. First, a simple definition. Some jobs every service must do, but they are not any service's real work. The Sidecar pattern moves such a job into a small, separate program. That program runs right beside the service, on the same machine. The service talks to its neighbour, and the neighbour talks to the outside world. Think of a busy restaurant kitchen. The chefs cook, and someone has to answer the phone. Teach every chef to answer it, and they all stop cooking, each with a different idea of the closing time. Put one person on the phone beside the kitchen, and when the closing time changes, you tell just that one person. In our online store, cards are charged in four places, through one payment provider. And one night, that provider has a bad three hundred milliseconds. By the end, you will know how four correct services can cause an incident nobody can be blamed for. What this pattern costs. And how it differs from the Decorator pattern.

2. The Scenario

Here is the scenario. The shop takes money in four places. Checkout charges a card while a customer watches. If it is slow, they notice. Refunds gives money back when a product is returned. Nobody is watching, but it must happen. Subscription billing runs at two in the morning, against thousands of saved cards, unattended. And marketplace payouts pays the independent sellers every Friday. Large amounts, and the sellers notice at once if it fails. Four teams. Four separate code bases. Four release days. And one payment provider behind all four. Remember that last part. Four unrelated programs, all talking to the same supplier.

3. Sixteen Copies of Four Decisions

None of the four teams wanted to become experts on the payment provider's network. But each had to answer the same four questions before going live. First: how many times should a failed attempt be retried? The provider sometimes declines everything for a fraction of a second, and then is fine. So the right answer is to wait a moment, and try again. But how many times, and how long? Second: when do you give up altogether? Third: which security settings does the connection use? And fourth: what do you count, and what do you call the counters? Four questions, four services: sixteen answers. And not one of them is about checkout, refunds, subscriptions, or payouts. They are all facts about a network, and a supplier's contract. Nobody copied carelessly. But once four versions exist, nothing brings them back together.

4. March — The Change Lands Three Times

First demo: in March, the change lands three times. The provider writes to all its customers. Retrying ten milliseconds after a failure does not help. So from now on: at most three attempts per payment, and wait properly between them. The shop's engineer does the obvious thing. Opens checkout, changes two lines, gets it reviewed, and releases it. Then refunds. Then marketplace payouts. Three changes, one afternoon, and everyone goes home. Three. Not four. Subscription billing was not updated. And nobody was careless. Billing runs overnight, lives in its own code base, and had no work planned that month. So nobody opened it. And nothing warns you. Every test in all four services still passes. Including billing's, because billing's own copy agrees with itself.

5. The Incident Is an Absence, Not a Mistake

Here is what that looks like in the code. Three of the four services have a method that applies the provider's new rules. It sets three attempts, and a two-hundred-millisecond wait. The fourth service simply does not have that method. Not a wrong version. Not an old version. It is not there at all. That matters. A wrong value could be spotted by comparing the four. But an absence has nothing to compare. Nothing in any build knows the fourth file exists. One test in this project checks that the method is missing. The failure we are about to hear is not caused by bad code. It is caused by code that was never written, in a place nobody looked.

6. Two In The Morning, Three Weeks Later

Second demo: two in the morning, three weeks later. The provider has one of its wobbles. For three hundred milliseconds it declines everything, and then it is fine. Here is the detail that turns a stale copy into an incident. During a wobble, the contract allows the shop twelve attempts in total. Three per payment, for four services. Subscription billing is already running, so it hits the wobble first. Using the old rules, it retries six times, quickly. And on the sixth, it gets through. It has spent six of the shop's twelve attempts. Then checkout pays, using three attempts. Then refunds, using three. That makes twelve. Marketplace payouts arrives fourth. It makes one attempt, and is refused. A hundred and eighty-six pounds forty does not reach the sellers. Now put two facts together. Subscription billing, the service with the old rules, the one that caused this, succeeded. As far as its team will ever know, the night went perfectly. And marketplace payouts, which is correct in every line, failed. Because it happened to arrive fourth.

7. The Pattern

Here is the pattern, in one sentence. Move the shared job out of the service, into a separate program running beside it, on the same machine. The service only talks to its neighbour. And the neighbour, called a proxy, is the only thing that goes out to the internet. Two phrases matter. Separate program. Not a library, and not a shared base class. And beside. Same machine, started and stopped together, one proxy per service. Not one shared proxy somewhere on the network. Why not just write a shared library? Often, that is the right answer, and we come back to it at the end. But a library does not fix this. Changing the rules still means a new version, and four teams upgrading. And the fourth team still has to open their code.

8. Who Does What

Let's name the pieces. They are all small. One interface, called takes payments. It asks for a name, and it pays a payment. Four classes implement it the old way. Each carries its own settings, and its own retry loop. Those are the sixteen copies, in code. A fifth version is almost empty. Its pay method has one line: hand the payment to the proxy. The proxy is a class called sidecar. It holds the retry loop the four services used to hold. But it does not own the numbers. It reads them from one shared settings object. All four proxies read the very same object. Not four equal copies, which would be March all over again. And underneath sits a payment gateway that fails on purpose. Every attempt count in this video is recorded by the gateway, at the receiving end. Never by a service counting its own attempts. Because a service's belief about how often it tried is exactly what was wrong.

9. What Is Left In The Service

Here is the code that matters, in words. The service's pay method is now one line long. Hand the payment to the proxy, and return what comes back. No retry loop, no counter, no wait, no deadline. They are not hidden elsewhere. They are gone. Next door, the proxy holds the loop that used to exist four times. But it reads its numbers from the shared settings. The proxy cannot change the rules. It is given them. And one more line, pointed out now so it is no surprise later. Before each attempt, the proxy waits one millisecond. That stands for the cost of hopping to the program next door, and back. It is there on purpose, because showing only the benefits would be a sales pitch.

10. The Same Night, With A Proxy Beside Each Service

Third demo: the same night, with a proxy beside each service. Nothing about the world has improved. The provider still has its bad three hundred milliseconds. The contract still allows twelve attempts. Subscription billing hands its payment to the proxy beside it. The proxy makes three attempts. It waits two hundred milliseconds, then four hundred. On the third attempt, the wobble has passed, and the card is charged. The service never knew there was more than one attempt. Checkout, the same. Refunds, the same. And marketplace payouts, which failed last time, pays the sellers on its third attempt. Twelve attempts, out of twelve allowed. Nobody refused. The rules were not applied four times, and missed once. They were stated once. We did not make four teams more careful. We made it impossible for four copies to exist.

11. The Bill, Part One — Twice As Many Things To Run

That is the pattern, and it works. Now the bill, with three items. Item one: counting. Copies of the shared rules fall from sixteen to four. Places to edit for one rule change fall from four to one. That is why you would do this. But programs to run and update rise from four to eight. You have doubled them. Now add a fifth service that takes payments. The old way, copies go from sixteen to twenty. With sidecars, still four. And one mistake to avoid. Retry counts, deadlines, and security settings are facts about the network. Whether a refund is allowed after ninety days is a fact about the shop. If that business rule ends up in a proxy's settings file, someone will search the whole refunds service for it. And it will not be there.

12. The Bill, Part Two — A Second Thing That Can Be Down

Item two: a second thing that can be down. The provider is fine. The network is fine. The service is fine. A customer is waiting to pay forty-seven pounds ninety-nine. But the proxy beside checkout failed to start after an update. The connection is refused. Attempts that reached the payment provider: zero. Not one attempt left the machine. And the service cannot fall back, because we deleted its retry code on purpose. So be honest about what happened. To make every call more reliable, you added a new dependency to every call. That is usually worth it. A proxy on the same machine, doing one small job, fails far less often than the internet. But the failure has a different shape. When the internet wobbles, one call fails, and the next may work. When a sidecar dies, every call that service makes fails, until someone restarts it. Rarer, but wider.

13. The Bill, Part Three — One Millisecond, On Every Call

Item three: the smallest number in this video. The same payment, the same rules, the same wobble, measured twice. With the retry code inside the service: three attempts, six hundred milliseconds. With the retry code in the proxy next door: three attempts, six hundred and three. Three milliseconds. One per attempt, for the hop next door, and back. On a payment that takes six hundred milliseconds, you would never notice. But on an internal call between two of the shop's own services, taking two milliseconds, one extra millisecond is a fifty percent increase. And if every service talks through a proxy, every hop is paid twice: once leaving, once arriving. So ask two questions about your system. How long does a typical call take? And how many hops does it make? Six hundred milliseconds and one hop, and this pattern is free. Two milliseconds and six hops, and it is not.

14. The Admission — This Is Decorator, Until You Deploy It

Now an honest admission. Everything in this video ran inside one Java program. And in one program, a proxy wrapped around a service is just one object wrapping another. That is the Decorator pattern. So what makes Sidecar different? Not the code. Where the code runs. A decorator is built into your program, written in your language. It changes when your service is rebuilt. A sidecar is its own program. It may be written in a language nobody on your team knows. It changes when someone restarts it, without your service ever being opened. That difference bought the one-place rule change. And it also cost the extra program, the extra failure, and the extra millisecond. So ask two questions. Must this job change without rebuilding the service? Must it work for services written in languages your library cannot support? Yes to either, and it goes next door. No to both, and a shared library inside your program is cheaper, faster, and one less thing to fail.

15. What to Take Away

Here are four things to remember. One. Count the copies, not the services. Four services, each holding four decisions, is sixteen things that can drift apart. If your biggest supplier changed its rules tomorrow, could you name every file you would need to open? Two. The failure lands away from the cause. The service that misbehaved stayed healthy all night. The one that failed was correct in every line. Three. You have met this pattern before, maybe without the name. A log shipper beside your application. A metrics agent on every machine. A web server handling encryption in front of your app. Each exists because the job is not your service's work, but it is everybody's problem. Four. What separates this from Decorator is where the code runs, not what it is. And one warning. No test inside any one service will ever notice the four have drifted apart. Because from inside each one, each one is right.
